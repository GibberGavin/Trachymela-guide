Methods and Material

Material

Much of the material used in this study has been collected by the author over a period of almost 50 years. This is supplemented by specimens from the Queensland Museum [QM], alongside material generously provided by other collectors over the years. These contributions are credited directly within the species descriptions and the acknowledgements.

Most field collecting was conducted using a standard beating tray placed beneath target vegetation, which was then shaken by hand or struck with a stick to dislodge insects. On windless, cool mornings, tapping the trunks of taller saplings with a small rubber mallet proved effective for dislodging beetles located higher up in the canopy. While some Trachymela individuals can be found at any hour, fieldwork seems to suggest they are significantly more active during the early morning hours.

Collected specimens were individually placed into glass livestock tubes, along with a small card labelled with the specimen's Accession Number. The Accession Number was recorded in a field note book, along with any pertinent notes (such as host identification). Accession numbers are of the form YYYY-nnn, where YYY is the year, and 'nnn' is a unique sequence number within the year (for example, "2004-214"). When multiple specimens were found on a single host plant, they were assigned the same Accession number, with the addition of a suffix "a", "b", etc. to distinguish individual specimens in that set. The location coordinates were determined using a GPS since 1999. Before then, locations were obtained from topographic maps.

An attempt was made to identify host trees to the species level. In early sampling periods, these identifications were often broad (e.g., recorded simply as "ironbark" or "bloodwood"). In recent years, more precise determinations have been made with the aid of the Euclid Identification Key. During the winter season when adults are inactive, more passive sampling methods—such as searching beneath loose bark or sorting through leaf litter at the base of host trees—proved useful. A mercury vapour (MV) light was deployed infrequently, as light-trapping provides no ecological information regarding host-plant associations; however, a few rare species in this guide were obtained exclusively using this method.

Recently collected specimens were photographed alive or fresh before pinning to preserve a record of their easily disturbed cuticular wax distribution. All specimens were measured for length, height, and width using either digital vernier calipers or an ocular micrometer graticule. Where possible, male specimens were dissected to extract the aedeagus shortly after collection.

Extracting the Aedeagus

In freshly killed males, the genitalia can usually be extracted by inserting a small pin (slightly bent at the tip) into the terminal abdominal segment, hooking the sclerotised aedeagus and gently pulling the structure outward. For older specimens, the beetle must first be softened by soaking in hot water for 15-30 minutes. The left elytron can then be carefully pried upward to allow a small lateral incision into the abdomen, through which the aedeagus is extracted using fine forceps or a bent pin. Returning the elytron to its natural resting position ensures minimal visible damage to the specimen.

The extracted aedeagus is cleared in a 10% KOH solution for roughly three hours at room temperature to dissolve attached soft tissue. The structure is then rinsed in distilled water for several hours, dehydrated in alcohol, and dried before being mounted on a card point pinned beneath the corresponding adult.

For specimens requiring imaging, the aedeagus was temporarily secured at its basal end with a tiny droplet of water-soluble glue to the tip of a micro-pin mounted on a high-density polyethylene "handle". This allowed the structure to be manipulated easily along multiple axes in front of the camera lens prior to permanent card mounting.

Photography

Photographs of live adults were obtained using a Panasonic G9 camera paired with an Olympus 60mm macro lens, supplemented by a Raynox DCR250 macro attachment for very small specimens. Lighting was controlled via a Godox TT350o flash unit paired with a Cygnustech Diffuser.

To achieve a sufficient depth of field for pinned specimens and extracted genitalia, automated focus-stacking was utilized. The stacking rig consisted of a WeMacro motorized rail controlled via its dedicated app, a Panasonic G9 body, a Laowa 2x ultra-macro lens, and up to three Meike macro extension rings as required. Studio lighting was obtained from two Godox TT350o flash units sync'd via a Godox X1R wireless trigger. While this configuration provided adequate magnification to capture the outlines of even the smallest structures (~1.2 mm aedeagus length), resolving fine internal details tested the limits of the optics. The resulting raw image stacks were compiled into single deep-focus composites using Helicon Focus software.

Data & Database Management

Locality and collection data are managed within a structured database. The core schema was built using BioLink—a platform developed by CSIRO Entomology between 1998 and 1999. Because BioLink has been legacy software for over a decade, it proved incompatible with modern Windows 11 architectures. To circumvent this without losing decades of data, a custom application was written in C# (termed BioDataLink) to directly interface with and maintain the legacy database tables. The development and syntax optimization of the BioDataLink codebase were assisted by the Google Gemini large language model.

The BioDataLink portal allows seamless record modification, updates to shifting species concepts, and data imports from community science platforms like iNaturalist. Additionally, it automates the generation of the distribution maps displayed throughout the key and species descriptions.

Representative series for each putative species were quantified across a standardized suite of metrics detailed later in this guide. Measurements were logged in an Excel spreadsheet file (Morphometrics.xlsm) using the unique specimen Accession Numbers. An R script (MorphoSum) combines data from both the database and spreadsheet to create statistical summaries and render diagnostic comparative charts of the data for each species.

Determination of Species

The primary diagnostic character relied upon for defining species limits within this guide is the structural morphology of the male aedeagus. While distinct genitalic differences are usually accompanied by subtle shifts in external morphometrics or color patterns, several "cryptic" sibling species share virtually identical external envelopes and can only be safely determined by male anatomy.

Conversely, situations arise where external morphospecies variations are distinct, yet share nearly identical male genitalia. Resolving these anomalies requires case-by-case taxonomic judgment to determine whether the population represents localized polymorphism or distinct sibling taxa; the rationale and limitations of these choices are explicitly detailed in the Notes section of each relevant species profile.

Skewed Field Sex-Ratios

Across the genus, collected specimens in a species are often heavily skewed toward females. Using an exact binomial test across the collected material, 23 species exhibit a statistically significant abundance of females over males (p < 0.05), whereas the inverse is true for only a single species. As a number of taxa are known from just one male specimen, it is likely that at least as many taxa are represented only by female specimens. Where such unique female specimens exhibit structural discontinuity clear enough to warrant distinction from known taxa, they have been treated as the baseline for a coded species.
